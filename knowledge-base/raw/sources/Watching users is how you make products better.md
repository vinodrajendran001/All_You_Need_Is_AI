Someone recently reached out to me for product feedback, and along with my comments I said: “One thing you’re probably not doing enough is watching real people use your product.”

That’s always been a fairly safe claim to make, because very few companies have ever invested enough in observing users. But when was the last time you heard someone mention something they learned by observing a real user of their product? I can only conclude people hardly do it anymore. (Notable exception: video game companies, where user experience is the whole point.)

Most sites and apps today are riddled with such glaring, frustrating problems that their creators must take great care to avoid ever watching real users. While execs pursue their dreams of AI riches, I doubt they’re going to spend time on boring details like confirming their vibecoded apps meet real needs. That would require acting as if other people mattered.

That’s too bad because, in my career in software design and engineering, the single most useful exercise I’ve ever done has always been just watching people use whatever product or feature I’m working on.

I’m not saying it’s a fun time. It was hard for me several years ago to watch a user struggle through a [Web Origami](https://weborigami.org/) tutorial and hear them say, “There are no words you could have written here that would have gotten me to understand this.” But that specific, uncomfortable experience led me to explore alternative ideas that resulted in a complete redesign of the language, and the project is better off for it today.

If you work at a big company that actually observes users (lucky you!), see if there’s any way you can join an observation session — reading a report or watching a video clip is nothing like being there live.

For everyone else, here are some suggestions for doing this at a small scale.

- It doesn’t need to be a big production. This week you could probably solicit someone who will let you observe them, and then it’s just a matter of finding an agreeable time.
- You’ll learn more if you go to them and observe them wherever they’d normally use your product: their home, their office, etc. When I was leading product design at Cozi, a company that makes software for families, I spent a lot of time observing users in their kitchens.
- Unless you’re testing a device, let them use their own device.
- Clarify up front that *you’re not testing them*, you’re testing the product. If they have trouble, that will not be their fault, it’ll be yours.
- Up front, get a *tiny* bit of information from them about who they are and why they use your product or might consider it. You just want enough context to understand how they might relate to your product.
- Ask them to think out loud as they go. Let them know that you’ll be typing notes as they go along.
- Give them a short script of basic tasks to do. If you’ve never done something like this before, just have them go through a few of your product’s basic functions. If they’re an experienced user, have them try whatever feature you’re working on.
- Focus on observing them do those tasks and taking notes. You’ll learn more the less you say.
- If they ask you a question, ask them what they would do if you weren’t there, then have them do that.
- If they get stuck, give them just a little time to see if they can figure things out. If it’s clear that they’re off the rails, you’re not going to learn anything more, and letting them suffer is cruel. Ask them to pause while you make a note of the problem, remind them that it’s not their fault, then help them get back on track.
- Before time runs out, ask them if they have any thoughts on how things went. If they don’t use your product yet, ask them whether they’d use it and, if not, why not.
- End the session on time. It’s fine if they don’t get through the script you gave them; you’ll have identified enough problems already.
- Pay them for their time. This could be cash, a gift card, a trial subscription, or anything that makes them feel like they were well compensated — they should be happy enough that they’d be willing to do it again.
- Observing users is something that benefits from repetition. If multiple people get stuck in the same place, you’ve found a real problem. If you design a fix for the problem, you can watch people try that to confirm the solution works.
- You don’t need big numbers of people to identify important issues.
- For more ideas, do a web search for “budget usability testing”.

I’ll be honest: observing someone try to use your product is humbling and often excruciating. No matter how much careful thought you’ve put into a product, watching a real user do realistic tasks will reveal all kinds of problems you’d have never guessed at. It will make you feel bad.

Some people have great difficulty acknowledging that the thing they helped make is the problem. At Microsoft in the 1990s, occasionally an engineer attending a usability lab test for the first time would make a disparaging comment along the lines of, “Where did you find such stupid users?” It could take such engineers a long time to reach the epiphany that the product they worked on had substantial design problems. It could take the engineer even longer to realize that *they themselves hadn’t spotted the problem* until the user ran into it. Maybe the stupid person isn’t the one on the mirrored side of the one-way mirror.

This is probably a fundamental reason why people steer clear of observing users. But fearing the awareness of shortcomings — and your own role in the process that led to those shortcomings — is childish. It’s a recipe for bad products, and you have so much to gain from a tiny investment of time in watching real people use your product.

If you’ve never done this, *why not try it once?* Literally every time you watch users will be extremly educational.
