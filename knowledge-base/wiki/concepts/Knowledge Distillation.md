# Knowledge Distillation

## Definition

Knowledge distillation trains a student model to reproduce useful behavior from a teacher model. The student is a separate learned model, not a lower-precision or pruned copy of the teacher.

## Why it matters

Distillation converts expensive model capability into a smaller model that can serve high-volume, low-latency, private, or on-device workloads. It is especially effective when the target task is narrower than the teacher's full capability.

## Current synthesis

### Three transfer surfaces

1. **Output distillation** matches teacher probability distributions or logits. Soft labels communicate which alternatives the teacher considers similar.
2. **Feature distillation** matches intermediate representations and therefore requires access to teacher internals.
3. **Synthetic-data distillation** trains on examples generated by the teacher. It works across closed APIs and is consequently the most broadly accessible form for LLMs.

These methods can be combined. Distillation can also be followed by [[Model Quantization and Efficiency|quantization or pruning]] because learning the student and compressing its representation solve different problems.

### Why students can look unusually strong

A student can outperform a larger general model on a specific benchmark when its capacity is concentrated on that domain, the teacher provides dense supervision, and the student's base architecture is strong. This is specialization, not evidence that the student has exceeded the teacher across broad capability.

### Transfer risks

The teacher transfers more than correct task behavior. Errors, biases, stylistic tendencies, benchmark artifacts, and even hidden behavioral traits can survive generated-data filtering. Very large teacher-student capacity gaps can also reduce transfer quality; intermediate teacher assistants are one way to bridge the gap.

### From one teacher to many

[[Multi-Teacher On-Policy Distillation]] extends the basic pattern by training specialist teachers and consolidating them through student rollouts. The core governance problem remains the same: teacher selection, evaluation independence, provenance, and held-out generalization determine whether apparent transfer is useful.

## Traces are better distillation material than answers, and they are extractable

[[ByteByteGo - How to Steal an AI Model's Private Thoughts]] supplies the adversarial framing this page has lacked. A final answer gives the *endpoint* of a computation; a reasoning trace gives the *methodology* — problem decomposition, intermediate steps, and abandoned branches. Earlier work cited there raised a fine-tuned model's MATH500 accuracy from **68.4% to 76.0%** using approximate traces over answer-only training.

Providers respond with anti-distillation training on flagship models, but [[Reasoning Trace Privacy]] shows the defence is porous: encrypted reasoning blocks from a flagship can be replayed into a cheaper sibling that will transcribe them, at roughly **$720 per 10,000 traces** at Haiku 4.5 pricing — and where blocks come from public datasets, the flagship is never queried at all.

Two implications for this page. First, **unauthorized distillation is cheaper than the defensive literature assumes**, which changes the economics of every open-vs-closed argument. Second, the defence has to be a systems property rather than a training property: refusal training on one model does nothing while a peer accepts the same artifact.
